# Inbound Call Discipline

Operating principles for the conversations the two engagement shapes are designed to attract. Applies to first contact through to scope/price agreement — Shape A (mandate) scoping, Shape B.1 (fractional CTO / CPO) discovery, Shape B.2 (transformation freelance) intros, and any advisory-shaped inbound that lands via LinkedIn or referral.

Not negotiation theatre. Discipline for staying in the frame the positioning was built to hold.

---

## The frame

These are **B2B sales conversations with long cycles**, not job interviews. The buyer is purchasing expensive judgment, not labor. That changes everything about how the conversation is run:

- Discovery comes before any anchor. Pricing, scope, availability — none of it lands until the problem and its value to the buyer are surfaced.
- The buyer needs internal champions for the engagement to close. The first call is rarely the deciding call; it's the call that determines whether a champion forms.
- The cycle nurtures over weeks, not minutes. Manufactured urgency is almost always a tell that the buyer hasn't decided yet and is testing pressure.

If the conversation drifts into "tell me about your background" Q&A, the frame has collapsed. Reset by asking a discovery question.

---

## Six principles

### 1. Discovery before anchor

Find the ceiling before naming a number. Surface the problem, its cost to the business, and what a solved version is worth before any pricing or scope discussion.

Default opening questions:
- What made you reach out *now* specifically?
- What gets you excited about the conversation?
- What does "this worked" look like in 6 months?
- What's the cost of the problem staying unsolved?

Anchoring early — on rate, on availability, on offer shape — gives away the only leverage the conversation has.

### 2. Talk to skin-in-the-game, not intermediaries

Recruiters and talent partners optimise for placement, not for a fit that compounds. Their information asymmetry is structural — they know what the company will pay; the candidate doesn't. The asymmetry only inverts in conversation with the decision-maker.

Default move when a recruiter is the entry point: be respectful, be brief, ask early to speak directly with the hiring principal or founder. If that's blocked, the role isn't ready.

### 3. Slow down. Information compounds; haste compresses leverage

Manufactured urgency ("we need to decide by Friday", "we're talking to others this week") almost always serves the buyer's process, not the work. The slower the conversation moves, the more information surfaces — about the real problem, about internal politics, about decision-makers, about what else has been tried.

Default response to urgency: name it calmly, propose a longer arc. *"This sounds like the kind of decision that benefits from a second conversation. What does next week look like?"*

### 4. The conversation is a consultancy, already underway

Frame the call as the first hour of the engagement, not a pitch for the engagement. Bring the same discovery posture, same trade-off thinking, same "here's what I'd want to understand first" that the actual work would bring.

This converts the buyer's perception from *"is this person credible?"* to *"this is what working with this person feels like."* The latter closes; the former negotiates.

### 5. Information + timing = leverage

The two variables that move outcomes. Everything else (logic, credentials, eloquence) is a distant second.

- **Information**: who else is in the conversation, what's been tried, what the real budget is, what the political constraint is, what the alternative to hiring is.
- **Timing**: when the decision actually has to be made (rarely the date stated), when the buyer's pain peaks, when a competing priority will pull their attention.

Surface both before any commitment. Withhold own constraints (availability, other conversations, pricing flexibility) until the buyer's are visible.

### 6. End every call with a feedback ask

Two questions, asked plainly, after the substantive part is done:

- *"What would you have loved me to ask that I didn't?"*
- *"Where do you think this conversation lands on a 1–10 of fit?"*

Both surface objections that would otherwise stay silent and shape the next call. The second is uncomfortable to ask — that's why it works.

## Anti-patterns to catch live

- **Pitching offers before discovery.** If the buyer hasn't articulated their problem yet, naming an offer shape is anchoring against an unknown ceiling.
- **Defending past work.** Future-oriented language closes; past-oriented language defends. *"What I'd do here"* > *"What I did three years ago"* once credibility is established.
- **Logic vs. emotion battles.** Information asymmetry favours the buyer. Pure logical argument loses. Move conversations into "what does this make possible for you" territory when logic stalls.
- **Email negotiation.** Body language and tone carry 70%+ of the signal. Move to video or in-person the moment terms are on the table. Email is for scheduling and confirming, not for deciding.
- **Confidence inflation in long messages.** The more confident the position, the shorter the message should be. A long justification reads as defending; a short statement reads as decided.

---

## When to walk

The frame protects against the wrong engagement, not just the wrong price. Walk when:

- The buyer can't articulate the problem after two discovery passes.
- The decision-maker refuses to enter the conversation directly.
- Urgency persists after being named — signal of either dysfunction or a manufactured close.
- The shape being asked for collapses into IC-under-a-lead work regardless of how it's framed.
- The conversation lives in Zone 3 of `credibility-map.md` (manager-of-managers at scale, hyperscale ops, deep-tech specialist depth) — refer out rather than push uphill.

Walking with care and curiosity preserves the relationship for a future conversation. Most "no" outcomes from this discipline return as "yes" outcomes 6–18 months later under different conditions.
